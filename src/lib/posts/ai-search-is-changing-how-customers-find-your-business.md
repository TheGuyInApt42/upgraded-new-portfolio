## Your Next Customer Might Never See Your Website — Until They Already Picked You

Picture this: someone in Mount Vernon needs a barber, an auto body shop, or a restaurant for tonight. A year ago, they typed it into Google, scrolled through a list of ten results, and clicked around until something looked right. That is not how it works anymore.

Now they ask a question — out loud to their phone, or typed into ChatGPT, or straight into Google — and an AI gives them an answer: three names, a sentence about each, and maybe a phone number. No list of ten links to browse. No scrolling. If your business is not one of those three names, you were never in the running. You did not lose the customer to a competitor with a better website. You lost them before they ever saw a website at all.

I heard the clearest version of this story last week. On the Niche Pursuits podcast, Anthony Eden — founder of DNSimple, a company doing around $7 million a year — said his search traffic fell by roughly 30% within about a month of Google rolling out AI Overviews. That is a healthy, established business losing nearly a third of its search visitors almost overnight, not because its website got worse, but because the search results page changed underneath it. If it can happen to a company with 19,000 customers, it can happen to a barbershop in Mount Vernon. The good news is that the fix is not complicated — and most of your competitors have not started on it yet.

## What Actually Changed

Search used to be a directory. You typed a query, Google showed you a list, and the best-optimized website won the click. Search is now an answer engine. The AI reads the web, summarizes what it finds, and hands the searcher a short answer. The click — the thing every local business website was built to win — is becoming optional.

The numbers back this up. A Semrush study found that AI Overviews now appear on **88% of commercial keywords** — the "best X," "X pricing," and "X near me" searches that used to send you customers — up from 46% a year earlier. And roughly **68% of U.S. Google searches now end without a click** to any website at all. Your customers have not stopped looking for local businesses. They have stopped browsing for them. The search now happens in places your website was never designed for:

- **Google AI Overviews** — the summary box at the top of Google results
- **ChatGPT** — where people ask for recommendations in plain language
- **Claude and Perplexity** — smaller, but growing fast as discovery tools
- **Voice assistants** — where there is only one spoken answer, not a list

That last one matters more than people realize. When someone asks their phone for "the best auto body shop near me," the assistant does not read out ten options. It reads out one or two. The businesses that get named are the ones the AI already knows, trusts, and can describe confidently.

## Why This Hits Local Businesses Differently

A big national brand can survive a traffic drop. It has millions of mentions across the web, a marketing team, and customers who already know its name. A local business has none of that cushion — and the new system makes that gap wider, not narrower.

### The Shortlist Effect

AI answers are short. Where Google used to show ten results, an AI summary names two or three businesses. Anthony Eden put it plainly in that same episode: for a smaller company, appearing inside those answers is hard when the biggest brands have far more mentions across the web. The AI does not rank you tenth and hope someone scrolls. It either mentions you or it does not.

That means the old local SEO game — rank on page one and wait — is no longer enough. Being fourth in the old results could still bring you calls. Being fourth in an AI answer brings you nothing, because the answer only has room for three.

### Mentions Matter More Than Rankings

Here is the shift that surprises the business owners I work with: AI systems do not just read your website. They read **everything the web says about you** — your reviews, your directory listings, local news mentions, your social media, what customers say about you on forums. Then they form an opinion.

A barbershop with 200 detailed Google reviews, a complete profile, and mentions on a few local sites is easy for an AI to describe: "highly rated, walk-ins welcome, known for fades." A shop with a nice website and twelve reviews is much harder to describe with confidence — so the AI picks the shop it can describe instead.

This is actually good news for good local businesses. You cannot outspend a chain on ads, but you can out-earn it on reputation. The businesses that win in AI search are the ones real customers talk about in specific, positive detail.

### The Questions Got More Specific

People do not search the way they used to. They ask full questions now, the way they would ask a friend: "Where can I get a same-day oil change near Mount Vernon?" "Which barber in the Bronx is best for a skin fade?" "What restaurant near me is good for a birthday dinner with ten people?"

Your website needs to answer those exact questions, in plain language, on the page. A services page that says "we offer quality haircuts" tells an AI nothing. A page that says "skin fades, beard trims, and walk-ins welcome — open Tuesday to Saturday, 9 to 7, on South 2nd Avenue in Mount Vernon" gives the AI everything it needs to recommend you.

## What to Do About It: The Stay-Discoverable Playbook

Nothing here requires a rebuild or a big budget. It is mostly doing the fundamentals properly — the same fundamentals that helped you in old-fashioned Google search, applied with AI answers in mind.

### 1. Treat Your Google Business Profile Like Your Storefront

Your Google Business Profile is now the single most important page you own, because it is the source AI systems trust most for local facts: your hours, your address, your services, your photos, your reviews. Make sure it is:

- **Complete** — every field filled in, services listed, attributes set
- **Current** — holiday hours updated, closed days marked, new services added
- **Visual** — recent photos of your actual shop, your team, your work
- **Active** — a post every week or two, even a short one

I wrote a full guide to fixing your profile in my Google Maps post — if you only do one thing from this article, do this one, because it feeds every AI answer about your business.

### 2. Get Reviews That Say Something Specific

A hundred five-star reviews that say "great place" help. Ten reviews that say "best fade in Mount Vernon, in and out in 30 minutes, fair prices" help far more — because AI systems quote specifics, not star counts.

Ask happy customers for a review while they are still in your chair, at your counter, or picking up their car. Make it easy: a QR code at the register, a link in a text message, a card they take home. And **reply to every review**, good or bad. Your replies are public, and they are part of what the AI reads when it decides whether to recommend you.

### 3. Answer Real Questions on Your Website

Add a page — or a section on your services page — that answers the questions customers actually ask, in their own words:

- "How much does a haircut cost?"
- "Do you take walk-ins?"
- "How long does a brake job take?"
- "Do you have parking?"
- "What neighborhoods do you serve?"

Write the question as a heading and answer it in two or three plain sentences. This is not keyword stuffing — it is the exact format AI systems look for when they build an answer, and it is genuinely useful to the humans who do click through.

### 4. Be Mentioned Beyond Your Own Website

AI trusts consensus. If your business shows up on your website and nowhere else, the AI has one source. If it shows up on your website, your Google profile, Yelp, local directories, a neighborhood blog, and a community Facebook group, the AI has a pattern — and patterns get recommended.

You do not need a PR firm for this. Get listed accurately in the directories that matter for your trade. Sponsor a local team or event and get your name on their page. Ask a happy regular to mention you in a neighborhood group. Every genuine mention is a vote the AI can count.

### 5. Keep the Basics Current and Consistent

Your name, address, and phone number should be identical everywhere — your website, your Google profile, every directory. AI systems cross-check, and conflicting information makes them less confident in recommending you. The same goes for your hours and your service list: if your website says you do something your profile does not mention, that service effectively does not exist to the AI.

### 6. Make Your Website Fast and Mobile-Friendly

AI systems still pull their information from the web, and they favor pages they can read quickly and cleanly. A slow, cluttered site that buries its phone number and hours is hard for an AI to use — and hard for the customers who do click through. I covered speed in detail last week, and it matters even more now: the visitors you do get from AI are further along in their decision, so your site needs to load instantly and make calling or booking effortless.

### 7. Be a Real Business With a Real Reputation

This is the part no shortcut replaces. DNSimple's answer to its 30% traffic drop was not a trick — Anthony Eden said the company doubled down on the foundations that built it: a good product, helpful current content, genuine customer advocacy, reputable review platforms, and traditional search visibility. AI systems still retrieve current information from web search engines, so conventional search presence still influences where brands show up.

The businesses that will do best in AI search are the ones that were already doing the work: showing up for customers, collecting honest reviews, keeping their information accurate, and being easy to find and contact. AI did not change what makes a business recommendable. It just made it easier to tell the difference between businesses that do the work and businesses that do not.

## What Not to Do

The AI gold rush has already attracted its share of bad advice. A few things to avoid:

- **Do not buy fake reviews.** AI systems and Google are both getting better at spotting them, and getting caught costs you the profile itself.
- **Do not stuff your pages with keywords.** "Best barber Mount Vernon best barber Bronx best barber NYC" reads like spam to humans and to AI alike. Write for a person asking a question.
- **Do not publish AI-generated content by the bucket.** A dozen thin pages of generic text will not make you the answer to anything. One genuinely helpful page will.
- **Do not pay for "GEO" magic.** Anyone selling a guaranteed spot in AI answers is selling something that does not exist yet. The fundamentals above are the strategy.
- **Do not send cold, AI-written outreach.** Eden mentioned this too — DNSimple barely uses cold email, and one attempt performed so poorly he never wanted it repeated. AI-written messages that pretend to know the recipient destroy trust, which is the one thing you cannot afford to lose right now.

## Final Thoughts

Search changed. It did not die. People still need barbers, mechanics, restaurants, and movers — and they still need to find them. What changed is the shape of the answer: shorter, summarized, and built from what the whole web says about you, not just what your website says.

That is a fairer game than the old one, if you play it. The old game rewarded whoever hired the best SEO person. The new game rewards whoever has the best reputation, the clearest information, and the most real customers saying real things about them. If that is your business, you are already ahead of most of your competitors — you just need to make sure the internet knows it.
